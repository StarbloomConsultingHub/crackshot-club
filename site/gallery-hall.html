The Gallery Games Hall

Step right up. Before the light gun and long before Duck Hunt, there were iron ducks on a chain, owls with a bullseye belly, bells that rang, clay pipes that shattered, and a Winchester chained to the counter. This hall enshrines the shooting galleries and carnival shooting games that Crack Shot is bringing back to the backyard.

How to read this hall. Every card lists its sources. Prices are auction results as published by the auction house or reported by the cited writer, with the date. They are not appraisals, and most iron targets carry no maker's mark, so attributions are the seller's. Where we couldn't verify a claim, we say so. Corrections with sources are welcome. See also the Smoothbore Hall.

How to walk: scroll or swipe, use ↑/↓ (or W/S), or hold Forward/Back. Use ←/→ or ⏮/⏭ to jump between exhibits, and click or tap a plaque (or press Read) to open its card. The Revival plaque waits at the end of the hall. Prefer a plain page? Choose List view.

Origins

Flobert Parlor Gun

Louis-Nicolas Flobert (Paris) and many European copyists · 1845 – c.1910

In 1845 Flobert pressed a lead ball into a percussion cap and gave it a rim: the 6 mm Flobert "bulleted breech cap," today's .22 BB Cap. With no powder, only the primer, it was quiet and gentle enough to shoot inside a large house, and Flobert built "parlor guns" for it. The CB Cap followed around 1888.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The first rimfire cartridge and the first gun built purely for indoor recreational target shooting. Every gallery rifle, and every .22 rimfire we shoot, descends from it.
Preservation & value
Flobert-type rifles and pistols still turn up in the antique trade; NRA's Shooting Sports USA (2024) notes they can be found with caveats about condition and ammunition. No dated sale price captured.

Sources: Wikipedia: Gallery gun · NRA Shooting Sports USA: Parlor Gun History (Art Merrill, Mar 28, 2024)

Origins

Shooting Parlors & the Wurfflein Shop

William Wurfflein, Philadelphia (in business 1869–1915) · 1870s – 1915

American indoor "shooting parlors" boomed again in the 1870s, giving Schuetzen-club target shooters a winter practice range. Philadelphia gunmaker William Wurfflein built parlor rifles and a new product line, mechanical gallery targets, and won a medal for his targets at the 1876 Centennial Exhibition. His 1889 catalog carried testimonials from gallery owners and trick shooters.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The bridge between the gentleman's parlor and the public gallery: purpose-built rifles plus mechanical, reacting iron targets sold to operators.
Preservation & value
Wurfflein targets realized $1,200 (owl) to $4,200 (folk-art cat) at Soulis Auctions, Sept 26, 2020; a Wurfflein bullseye with drop-down bird brought $2,200 there.

Sources: American Society of Arms Collectors: The Wurffleins of Philadelphia (1998, PDF) · Soulis Auctions: "World's Greatest Shooting Gallery Target Auction" (Tucker collection), prices realized, Sept 26, 2020

Target maker

Quackenbush Bell Target

H.M. Quackenbush Co., Herkimer, New York · 1880s – 1930s

Henry Quackenbush, a former Remington apprentice, began making air rifles in 1876 and mass-produced "gallery guns" in the 1880s; his name appeared in shooting galleries across the country. His signature mechanical target was a heavy 12-inch concentric bullseye: hit the center and a bell rang and a painted steel duck popped up.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
Proof that the "ding!" of a reacting target was a selling point from the start, and a maker who served both galleries and backyard air-rifle shooters.
Preservation & value
The Quackenbush factory site was added to the New York State and National Registers of Historic Places in June 2023, with plans for a museum. Bell targets appear at auction regularly; no dated sold price captured.

Sources: Wikipedia: Henry Quackenbush · Lemelson Center (MIT): Henry Quackenbush · Invaluable: Quackenbush mechanical cast-iron gallery target (lot listing)

Gallery rifle

Winchester Model 1890 "Gallery Gun"

Winchester Repeating Arms Co.; design by John & Matthew Browning · 1890 – 1932 (stock sold to 1941)

A slide-action, top-ejecting .22 with a tube magazine, chambered for one round each: .22 Short, Long, or WRF (Long Rifle only from 1919). About 849,000 were made. For years it was the standard rifle chained to the counter of American shooting galleries; the 1906 in .22 Short joined it.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The rifle most Americans actually fired at a gallery for half a century. "Gallery gun" became its nickname.
Preservation & value
Common enough to collect, with real gallery-used guns showing counter-tie-down wear. No dated sale captured; see the Winchester Collector forum for how to tell a genuine gallery gun.

Sources: Wikipedia: Winchester Model 1890 · Gun Digest: Classic Firearms: Winchester Model 62 (May 2019) · Winchester Collector forum: "Gallery Guns" (2006)

Gallery rifle

Winchester Model 62 Gallery

Winchester Repeating Arms Co. · 1932 – 1958

The improved successor to the 1906: visible hammer, 23-inch barrel, S/L/LR interchangeable, or .22 Short only for galleries. Gallery models usually carry a big "WINCHESTER" roll-mark on the left receiver (sometimes in red) and a large triangular loading port for the operator's pre-filled 10-shot tubes.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The last factory gallery Winchester. Only about 3% of more than 409,000 Model 62/62As were .22 Short, which is why marked gallery guns are prized.
Preservation & value
Gallery-marked 62As are sought by collectors (Gun Digest, 2019; Winchester Collector forum). No dated sale price captured.

Sources: Gun Digest: Classic Firearms: Winchester Model 62 (May 2019) · Winchester Collector forum: "Gallery Guns" (2006)

Target maker

W.F. Mangels Co., Coney Island

William F. Mangels, "Wizard of Eighth Street," Coney Island, NY · c.1886 – 1969

A German immigrant machinist who by 1886 ran a Coney Island shop casting iron targets for shooting galleries, later famous for carousel mechanisms and rides like the Whip. Collectors credit Mangels with more shooting-gallery target patents than anyone, and his shop kept making targets until 1969, long after rivals quit. His Catalogue #4 sold whole mechanical galleries with steel and iron birds, ducks, boats, and clay pipes.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The defining American gallery maker, and the historian of the trade: he founded the American Museum of Public Recreation (1929) and wrote The Outdoor Amusement Industry (1952).
Preservation & value
Top prices: the only known Mangels light-up clown "Rowdy" $32,000 and the only known Mangels crocodile $20,000 (Soulis, Sept 26, 2020). A Mangels paratrooper brought $1,020 (Morphy, Apr 2012). Whole galleries: $43,200 (Duke Farms gallery, 2009) and $60,000 (150+ targets, Elli Buk collection, May 2013), per Amusing the Zillion.

Sources: Coney Island History Project: William F. Mangels (Hall of Fame) · Coney Island History Project: W.F. Mangels Co. Catalogue #4, p. 20, Mechanical Shooting Gallery · Amusing the Zillion: Coney Island Shooting Gallery from 1940s Makes Comeback (Feb 28, 2013) · Amusing the Zillion: William F. Mangels posts (incl. 2012 Morphy paratrooper) · Amusing the Zillion: Step Right Up! (Tucker, Schiffer 2014) and Mangels gallery sale · Soulis Auctions: "World's Greatest Shooting Gallery Target Auction" (Tucker collection), prices realized, Sept 26, 2020 · Auction Daily: Soulis presents the Tucker reference collection (2020 preview)

Target maker

C.W. Parker, "The Amusement King"

C.W. Parker Amusement Co., Abilene, then Leavenworth, Kansas · 1880s – 1955

Charles W. Parker got into show business by buying a traveling shooting gallery, then building a better one. He went on to build about 1,000 carousels and whole carnivals, but kept making galleries; a c.1910 "Catalogue of Shooting Galleries" survives. Parker targets are known for rocking, animated, and knock-down-rider mechanisms.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The shooting gallery was literally the seed of the biggest carnival-equipment maker west of the Mississippi.
Preservation & value
At Soulis (Sept 26, 2020): Parker Abilene whippet $17,000, animated owl $15,000, Indian princess $3,600, rocking target $3,000. Parker's Leavenworth carousel museum is open weekends.

Sources: Wikipedia: C. W. Parker · Visit Abilene / Dickinson County Historical Society: The Amusement King (Dec 16, 2020) · Leavenworth Historical Museum Assn.: C.W. Parker Carousel Museum · Soulis Auctions: "World's Greatest Shooting Gallery Target Auction" (Tucker collection), prices realized, Sept 26, 2020 · Antique Trader: Shooting Gallery Targets at Soulis Auction

Target maker

The Chicago & L.A. Foundries

H.C. Evans, Emil Hoffmann, Mueller, A.J. Smith (Chicago); J.T. Dickman (Los Angeles) · c.1900 – 1930s

Chicago was the midway supply capital. H.C. Evans, Emil Hoffmann, Mueller and A.J. Smith cast targets and sold them through trade catalogs; out west, J.T. Dickman patented his "Great Clown Target with the Bright Eye" in 1911 and listed it in his 1921 Catalog E. Most targets carry no maker's mark, so catalogs and trade cards are how they are attributed.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
Rival foundries pushed the art: Man-in-the-Moon gongs, kicking mules, illuminated eyes, card-suit spinners.
Preservation & value
Soulis (Sept 26, 2020): only known H.C. Evans Man in the Moon $15,000; Evans kicking mule $6,000; Dickman heart card-suit $7,500; Hoffmann illuminated-eye $2,800; owl attributed to A.J. Smith $1,300.

Sources: Amusing the Zillion: Step Right Up! (Tucker, Schiffer 2014) and Mangels gallery sale · Soulis Auctions: "World's Greatest Shooting Gallery Target Auction" (Tucker collection), prices realized, Sept 26, 2020 · Antique Trader: Shooting Gallery Targets at Soulis Auction · Auction Daily: Soulis presents the Tucker reference collection (2020 preview)

Target types

The Iron Menagerie: Ducks, Owls, Bells, Spinners & Pipes

Many makers, mostly unmarked · c.1880 – 1950s

The vocabulary of the gallery: a conveyor row of knock-down ducks; owls with a bullseye belly; bell and gong targets that ring on a hit; star and card-suit spinners; rotating wheels of white clay pipes that shatter; and animated rabbits, deer and soldiers that drop, swing or light up. Mangels' catalog lists birds, ducks, boats and clay pipes in one gallery.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
This is the direct ancestor of the Crack Shot cracker: a cheap, breakable, satisfying target that tells you instantly that you hit.
Preservation & value
Everyday pieces are attainable: duck-form targets $300–$450, ordinary iron owls $425–$1,800, a Mangels rotating target with pipes $2,000 (Soulis, Sept 26, 2020). Many galleries were scrapped in WWI/WWII metal drives (Amusing the Zillion).

Sources: Soulis Auctions: "World's Greatest Shooting Gallery Target Auction" (Tucker collection), prices realized, Sept 26, 2020 · Coney Island History Project: W.F. Mangels Co. Catalogue #4, p. 20, Mechanical Shooting Gallery · Amusing the Zillion: William F. Mangels posts (incl. 2012 Morphy paratrooper)

Electric age

Seeburg Ray-O-Lite Rifle

Rayolite Rifle Range Co. (Tulsa) / J.P. Seeburg Corp., Chicago · 1935 – 1950s

The first light-gun game. A rifle fires a beam of light; a photo-tube on a flying duck drops it when hit. Shown in 1935, advertised 1936 at $495 with 10–20 shots for a nickel. Seeburg followed with Chicken Sam (1939), Jailbird (1940), Shoot the Bear (1947) and Coon Hunt (1952).

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
It moved the gallery indoors and away from lead: same duck, same thrill, no bullet.
Preservation & value
Collected and restored by EM-arcade hobbyists (Pinball Repair, Sands Mechanical Museum). No dated sale price captured.

Sources: Pinball Repair (EM Arcade History): 1936 Seeburg Ray-O-Lite · The Sands Mechanical Museum: Rayolite advertisements and patents · The Golden Age Arcade Historian: The First Light Gun Game (May 2015)

Electric age

Chicago Coin Rifle Galleries

Chicago Coin Machine Co. (Chicago Dynamic Industries) · 1961 – 1976

Coin-op "shooting galleries in a box." The 1961 Long Range Rifle Gallery used a mirror to make clown and circus targets look 15 feet away; the 1962 World's Fair Rifle Gallery used double mirrors for 20 feet. Later came black-light Super Scope (1966), Apollo Moon Shot (1969), Sharp Shooter (1971) and, fittingly, Coney Island Rifle (1976).

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The midway gallery, miniaturized for every arcade, bowling alley and boardwalk, kept the form alive as live-fire galleries faded.
Preservation & value
Working examples are shown at collections like the Silverball and Knapp arcades and the Torrence Collection. No dated sale price captured.

Sources: Pinball Repair (EM Arcade History): Chicago Coin gun games list · Torrence Collection: Chicago Coin Long Range (1961) and World's Fair (1962) Rifle Galleries · Museum of the Game (KLOV): Chicago Coin Machine Co. machine list

Electric age

Sega & Nintendo: Projection and Laser Clay

Sega (Japan); Nintendo Laser Clay Shooting System (1973) · 1968 – 1970s

In 1968–69 Sega built electro-mechanical gun games with rear-projected moving targets and mirrored depth, starting with its own Duck Hunt (released Jan 1969). Nintendo turned failing bowling alleys into Laser Clay Shooting System ranges in 1973 and later built electro-mechanical light-gun games on the same idea.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
Clay pigeons, thrown targets, a light gun: the moment the gallery started to look like skeet, the same leap Crack Shot makes with crackers.
Preservation & value
Surviving machines are rare museum and collector pieces. No dated sale price captured.

Sources: Wikipedia: Electro-mechanical game (Sega gun games, 1968–69) · Wikipedia: Duck Hunt

Pop culture

Duck Hunt (NES)

Nintendo (dir. Shigeru Miyamoto; prod. Gunpei Yokoi) · 1984 – today

Released April 1984 in Japan and as a Vs. arcade game, then as a launch title for the NES with the Zapper light gun in October 1985. Three shots per round at ducks, plus a "Clay Shooting" mode. Over 28 million copies sold, and one infamous laughing dog.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The pop-culture descendant of every iron duck row. It is why "shooting gallery" still means something to people born after the galleries closed, and it is the model for our Crack Shot browser game.
Preservation & value
Still played on original hardware, which needs a CRT TV for the Zapper to work. Nintendo trademarks belong to Nintendo; Crack Shot is not affiliated.

Sources: Wikipedia: Duck Hunt · Wikipedia: NES Zapper

Decline

Disneyland's Galleries

Disneyland (Anaheim) and Magic Kingdom · 1955 – 1985

Disneyland opened in 1955 with a Main Street shooting gallery (to 1962), then an Adventureland Safari gallery (1962–1982), plus Frontierland's. Targets had to be repainted nightly because the shot chipped the paint. In 1985 the Frontierland Shootin' Arcade replaced its lead-pellet guns with infrared beams, as Florida had done.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
A clear marker of the turn: America's most famous gallery kept the game but dropped the projectile.
Preservation & value
Frontierland Shootin' Exposition still operates with infrared guns (Duchess of Disneyland, 2015; Yesterland).

Sources: Duchess of Disneyland: Disneyland's original shooting galleries (Jan 9, 2015) · Yesterland: Frontierland Shootin' Arcade

Decline

The Decline: Scrap, Lead & Liability

— · 1940s – 1980s

Iron galleries were sold for scrap in the World War metal drives or broken up so targets could be sold one by one. Live .22 galleries gave way to air guns, light guns and infrared. Lead exposure at indoor ranges became a documented public-health issue from the 1970s on, with a 1990 NRA official quoted as saying lead contamination had closed hundreds of indoor ranges in 20 years.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
Knowing why the galleries vanished is how a revival avoids the same traps: outdoors, safe backstops, low-lead or no-lead targets, and adults only.
Preservation & value
Intact iron galleries are "exceptionally rare" (Amusing the Zillion, 2013). We could not verify a specific law that banned live-ammo carnival galleries; treat claims of one with caution.

Sources: Amusing the Zillion: Coney Island Shooting Gallery from 1940s Makes Comeback (Feb 28, 2013) · Violence Policy Center: Poisonous Pastime, sec. 1 (quotes a 1990 NRA official on lead and range closures; advocacy source) · Seattle Times: Loaded with lead (2014)

Preservation

Keepers of the Midway

Collectors, museums and historians · 1980s – today

Richard and Valerie Tucker began collecting with a row of Mangels doves in the early 1980s and wrote the only book on the subject, Step Right Up! Classic American Target and Arcade Forms (Schiffer, 2014). Their collection was sold at Soulis Auctions on Sept 26, 2020. The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History holds mechanical gallery targets in its collection; San Francisco's Musée Mécanique keeps 300+ playable coin-op machines; the Coney Island History Project preserves Mangels catalogs.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
Documentation raises value and saves pieces from the scrap pile. Crack Shot wants to add an owners' registry and photo archive to that work.
Preservation & value
See the price notes on each maker. Prices quoted are auction results as published by the auction house; they are not appraisals.

Sources: Amusing the Zillion: Step Right Up! Revival

The Revival

Coney Island USA; backyard clubs like Crack Shot · 2013 – today

After Superstorm Sandy, a 1940s Mangels gallery that had stood under the Wonder Wheel was pulled out of storage at Deno's, restored, and reopened in August 2013 on Surf Avenue at Coney Island USA, believed to be the only one of its kind open to the public. Meanwhile, the Henry Garden Gun keeps a smoothbore .22 in production, and a tray of round crackers costs less than a box of clays.

Why it matters, value & sources
Why it matters
The gallery never died; it just needs new shooters. That is what this club is for.
Preservation & value
Coney Island USA gallery: in operation as of the 2013 reports. Check before you visit.

Sources: Amusing the Zillion: Coney Island Shooting Gallery from 1940s Makes Comeback (Feb 28, 2013) · Amusing the Zillion: Step Right Up! (Tucker, Schiffer 2014) and Mangels gallery sale

A short timeline

  1. 1845: Flobert's 6 mm BB cap and "parlor guns" make indoor target shooting possible.
  2. 1870s: American shooting parlors boom; Wurfflein's mechanical targets win a medal at the 1876 Centennial.
  3. c.1886: W.F. Mangels is casting iron gallery targets in Coney Island.
  4. 1880s–1890s: Quackenbush gallery guns and bell targets; C.W. Parker builds galleries in Kansas; Winchester's Model 1890 becomes "the gallery gun."
  5. 1900s–1930s: The Chicago foundries (Evans, Hoffmann, Mueller, A.J. Smith) and J.T. Dickman in Los Angeles fill trade catalogs with ducks, owls, moons and clowns.
  6. 1932–1958: Winchester Model 62 Gallery, the last factory gallery Winchester.
  7. 1935–1936: The Rayolite Rifle Range / Seeburg Ray-O-Lite, the first light-gun game.
  8. 1961–1976: Chicago Coin's coin-op rifle galleries; Sega's projection gun games (1969); Nintendo's Laser Clay ranges (1973).
  9. 1969: Mangels stops making targets.
  10. 1984–1985: Duck Hunt and the Zapper; Disneyland's Frontierland gallery switches from lead pellets to infrared.
  11. 2013: A restored 1940s Mangels gallery reopens at Coney Island USA.
  12. 2014–2020: Step Right Up! is published, and the Tucker collection sells at Soulis Auctions.

What we checked and couldn't confirm

  • Smith & Wesson as a target maker: no evidence found. S&W matters to galleries because of the .22 Short cartridge, not targets. The "Smith" in target-maker lists is A.J. Smith of Chicago.
  • Parker: confirmed. It's C.W. Parker of Abilene and Leavenworth, Kansas, not the shotgun maker.
  • Wurlitzer light-gun or rifle galleries: none found. The electric-gallery names we could document are Seeburg, Chicago Coin, Sega and Nintendo.
  • "American Coin-Op Museum": we couldn't find a museum by that exact name. We list the Smithsonian NMAH, Musée Mécanique, the Coney Island History Project, and the C.W. Parker Carousel Museum instead.
  • A specific law banning live-ammunition carnival galleries: not found. The record shows a gradual switch to air, light and infrared guns, plus lead concerns at indoor ranges.
