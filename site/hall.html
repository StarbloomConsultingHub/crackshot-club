The Smoothbore Hall

A hall of fame for the little smoothbore .22s, and a few cousins, built to throw a pinch of No. 12 shot at things that fly. They were made for boys' rifles, barnyard rats, Mo-Skeet-O and Targo, and now crackers. Many are still out there at fair prices if you know the rollmarks.

How to read this hall. Dates come from collector references and often disagree; we show the disagreement instead of hiding it. Values are snapshots with a source and date, not appraisals. "Unverified" means thin or secondhand data. Corrections with sources are welcome. Nothing here is gunsmithing advice, and the club never buys, sells, or trades guns.

A short history in eight shots

  1. 1845: the Flobert cap. Louis-Nicolas Flobert seats a lead ball on a percussion cap. The result is the 6 mm Flobert, or .22 BB Cap: the first rimfire cartridge, with no powder, just primer. "Flobert," "parlor," and "saloon" guns follow for indoor shooting. The slightly stronger .22 CB Cap arrives around 1888. (SSUSA; Wikipedia)
  2. 1890s–1920s: boys' rifles go smooth. Stevens builds about 250,000 falling-block "boys' rifles." The Favorite No. 20 and the Crack Shot No. 26½ are factory smoothbores for .22 and .32 rimfire shot cartridges. In 1902 a Crack Shot cost $2.25. (Wikipedia)
  3. 1890–1958: the gallery gun. Winchester's 1890, 1906, and 62 pumps become the shooting-gallery standard. The true Gallery models were rifled .22 Short guns with a big triangular loading port, a counter tie-down, and frangible "Spatterpruf" ammo, shot at cast-iron ducks on Mangels and Parker midways. Live-.22 galleries lasted into the 1950s at places like Coney Island (Winchester Collector; Gun Digest).
  4. 1920–1927: Winchester Model 36. A 9 mm Flobert smoothbore, sold as a garden gun. It's a cousin of the .22s.
  5. 1930s: Mo-Skeet-O. Fred Routledge of Monroe, Michigan, invents a reduced-scale clay game and a stepped "Routledge bore." Remington (121, 510/511/512, later 514) and Winchester (61, 67) build smoothbores for it. The Depression, and then wartime shotshell shortages, make .22 "shotguns" a hit.
  6. 1938–1946: Mossberg Targo. Smoothbore bolt guns (26T, 42T, 42TR, B42T), a barrel-mounted Model 1A trap, and 2 11/16-inch birds. "Zing!… out sails the target. Bang, goes the gun."
  7. 1961–1979: revival and fade. Mossberg relaunches Targo (320TR, 340TR) as the NRA ".22 Shotgun Qualification Course" and drops it within about two years as "too difficult and discouraging." Remington's 572SB pump runs until 1979, and the Nylon 10 SB shows up from 1962 to 1964.
  8. 1999–today: the garden gun. Marlin's 25MG (1999–2002) is a .22 WMR snake-shot gun. In 2019 Henry's H1 Garden Gun arrives, and it's still the only smoothbore .22 in production that we know of. And then, Crack Shot.

Honorable mention: the .32 smoothbores

Yes, there really was a .32. What's confirmed: Stevens catalogued its boys' rifle smoothbores for .32 rimfire shot cartridges as well as .22. The Favorite No. 20 was "chambered for the 22 or 32 rimfire shot cartridges and has a smoothbore barrel and no rear sight" (Standard Catalog of Firearms, 2001, as quoted). Wikipedia lists the Crack Shot No. 26½ for ".22LR Shot Cartridges, .32 Rimfire Shot Cartridges." So the gun that gave our club its name came in .32 shot too.

Ammo: .32 rimfire shot loads did exist. A Canadian collector reports shooting a Favorite No. 20 with CIL .32 Long Shot (#10, 1935-dated) (CGN forum). That's a single collector's report.

Likely but unverified: Remington's No. 4 rolling block. A forum post cites Fjestad's Blue Book that "the .32 smoothbore was introduced around 1911" (forum). A Rimfire Central post says the Standard Catalog lists No. 6 smoothbores (forum). Both are secondhand, so we're waiting on a catalog page before we call them confirmed.

Sources: Wikipedia: Stevens Boys Rifles · SASS Wire, 18 Jan 2012. A .32 rimfire smoothbore's chamber isn't interchangeable with a .22's, and original .32 rimfire ammunition is scarce, collectible, and old. Treat these as display pieces unless a gunsmith says otherwise.

Famous, but not smoothbores

These get mistaken for shot guns all the time. They're rifled, but they belong to the same story.

Winchester 1890, 1906 and 62/62A "gallery guns"

The classic midway pumps. Real gallery models were rifled, chambered for .22 Short only, with a large triangular loading port for pre-loaded gallery tubes and often a counter tie-down. Galleries fed them frangible .22 Shorts such as Western Spatterpruf. Gun Values says Gallery 62s bring a 300% premium.

Winchester Collector forum: What makes one a 62A Gallery? · Gun Digest: Classic Firearms: Winchester Model 62 · Gun Values: Winchester 62 and 62A

Winchester 1885 "Winder Musket"

A rifled .22 Short single-shot trainer built on the 1885 action and named for Col. C.B. Winder. U.S. Ordnance bought them for marksmanship training until the 1922 bolt-action trainer replaced them. It's a .22 gallery-era cousin, but not a smoothbore.

Guns International listing: 1919/1920 Winder Musket · Winchester Collector forum: Model 1885 Winder

Winchester 1873 in .22 rimfire

Winchester did make the '73 in .22 rimfire. It was rifled, not a shot gun. We haven't verified production details for this page.

Savage / Stevens Model 72 "Crackshot"

A 1970s–80s Savage revival of the falling-block boys' rifle that reused the Crackshot name. It's rifled, with used prices around $400–$500 (TrueGunValue).

TrueGunValue: 72 Crackshot · Rimfire Central: Savage Model 72

Rossi / Taurus Model 62 and Rossi Gallery

Modern copies of the Winchester 62 pump, all rifled. We found no factory smoothbore barrel for them.
